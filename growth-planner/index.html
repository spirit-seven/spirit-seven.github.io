What is compound interest?

Compound interest means earning growth on your earlier growth as well as on the money you paid in. Each year’s growth is added to the balance, so the next year’s growth is worked out on a bigger amount. Over a few years the effect is small, but over decades it can make up more of the balance than the money you paid in.

At 1% a year, the fees charged come to £17,592, but the balance ends up £27,627 lower. The gap is bigger because money taken as fees is no longer there to grow.

Why show today’s money?

A figure 25 years from now won’t buy what the same figure buys today. In the example, £180,297 in 25 years would buy about what £109,897 buys now, if inflation averages 2% a year. The “today’s money” figure divides the balance by inflation to that date, so you can judge it against prices you know.

How it works

The balance grows monthly at the yearly growth rate you enter, so 12% growth turns 1,000 into exactly 1,120 over a year. Fees are taken monthly as a share of the balance, and payments go in at the end of each month. “Today’s money” divides by inflation to that date, so it shows what the balance would buy now.
